*Inception Edition · May 11, 2026 · A Short Tour of the Technology*

---

An autonomous trading system has been running for two weeks against a US-equities paper
account. Four decision cycles per market session, no human in the loop, every cycle written
to a structured journal. Every Sunday a second model reads that journal end-to-end and
produces a critique that gets folded into the following week's prompt.

This is the launch post — a short tour of how the parts fit together, before the weekly
recaps start.

---

## 🧩 The Stack in One Sentence

Anthropic's **Claude** does the reasoning, **Python** does the math and the policing, and
**Alpaca** holds the (paper) brokerage account where orders actually live.

Three layers, three jobs:

- **The brain.** A Claude model reads a structured snapshot of the market — prices, momentum,
  volatility, news context, and the current state of the account — and returns a JSON
  decision: `BUY`, `SELL`, or `HOLD`, with a size, a stop-loss, and a one-line rationale.
- **The referee.** A deterministic Python module — no AI involved — reads that decision and
  either approves it, clamps it, or throws it out. Position too big? Trimmed. Symbol not on
  the allowlist? Rejected. Confidence below threshold? Held. Daily loss limit hit? Shut down.
- **The executor.** Once the rules sign off, the order goes to Alpaca as a **bracket order**:
  entry, stop-loss, and take-profit all attached at the broker. If the bot crashes mid-trade,
  the protective stop still lives on the exchange. The bot's bad day cannot drag the account
  with it.

A few things worth noticing in that picture:

- **Indicators are computed in Python**, not asked from Claude. RSI, MACD, EMA, ATR,
  Bollinger bands — all classic, all deterministic, all the same answer every time given the
  same input. The reasoning model is not allowed anywhere near the math.
- **Claude only sees the snapshot.** It has no free internet access, no direct line to the
  broker, no ability to "look something up." It reads a tidy JSON object and returns a tidy
  JSON object. That's it.
- **The risk engine is the last word.** Whatever Claude proposes, the deterministic policy
  layer gets the final stamp.
- **The journal closes the loop.** The week's decisions feed Sunday's review, and the
  review feeds the following week's system prompt. Memory, in other words, is mandatory.

The whole cycle, end-to-end, takes a few seconds per symbol.

## 🛡️ Why a Brain *and* a Rulebook

Most trading bots are pure rules. A few are pure AI. This one is deliberately both — and the
seam between them is the point.

Reasoning models are good at reading nuance. A stock gapping up on earnings while RSI says
"overbought" is the kind of paragraph an LLM can interpret thoughtfully. But reasoning models
also occasionally make stuff up: a fabricated ticker, an over-confident call, a 50% position
size that appears out of thin air. That is exactly the kind of mistake a deterministic rule
layer is built to catch.

The interesting case is **clamping**. The rules don't always reject — they often just *trim*.
An 18% position request becomes a 5% position. A 0.62 confidence becomes a `HOLD` with a
logged reason. The AI proposes; the policy disposes. Neither side gets to be the dictator.

Other rules that live in that same Python module: a daily loss circuit breaker (–2% equity →
halt all new trades for the day), a weekly drawdown pause (–5% → no new positions for a full
week), a hard kill switch at –15% from peak, a cooldown after stop-outs, defined-risk-only on
options, and a reconciliation check every cycle to confirm the bot's view of the portfolio
matches what the broker actually says.

## 📓 Memory Is Mandatory

Every cycle writes a structured record to a SQLite database and a JSONL journal:

- The market snapshot it saw
- The decision Claude returned
- What the risk engine did about that decision
- What Alpaca said when (or whether) the order went out

That journal is not just a debugging tool — it is the input to the **weekly review**. Every
Sunday, a different Claude model (Sonnet — slower, more thoughtful, more expensive) reads the
entire week of journal entries and writes a critique: what worked, what looked like luck,
what patterns are showing up, what to adjust. The next week's runs go out with that feedback
folded into the system prompt.

It is not quite self-improvement. It is something humbler: a system that reads its own
performance review before the next exam.

## 💸 The Money Is (Still) Fake

The bot trades a **$100,000 paper trading account**. Simulated dollars. Real market data,
real prices, real spreads, real fills — but nothing of value at risk. Think of it as a
flight simulator for algorithmic trading: the instruments are real, the physics are real,
but a tailspin doesn't hurt anyone.

The plan is at least 60–90 days of paper trading before anyone touches a "go live" button,
and even then the live account starts an order of magnitude smaller than the paper one. Year
one is not about beating the S&P 500. It is about earning trust slowly, with a track record
long enough to actually mean something.

## 🧠 What's Actually Interesting About This

The honest answer to "why build this?" is that nobody quite knows yet how a reasoning model
behaves when it is dropped into a real-feedback loop, week after week, with a journal of its
own decisions to learn from.

- Does it get better over time, or does it drift?
- Does it overcorrect after a losing day?
- Does it develop habits — symbols it gravitates toward, setups it ignores?
- Can a deterministic rule layer keep an AI honest in production for months on end?

Two weeks of paper data is not enough to answer any of that. Twelve weeks might start to.
That, more than the P&L curve, is the actual experiment.

## 📅 What This Blog Will Cover

Every Sunday this page gets a new post. It is generated by the same weekly review process
that feeds the bot — but rewritten in plain English: no JSON, no jargon, no indicator
readings. Just a readable account of what the bot did, what the numbers look like, what
patterns are showing up, and what is changing for next week.

The charts further down a post will start populating once there is a full week of post-launch
data behind it.

Next Sunday's post will be the first real recap — actual decisions, actual outcomes, actual
patterns worth talking about. It probably won't be perfect. It might be entertainingly wrong
